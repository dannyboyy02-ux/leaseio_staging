Lease Lock / Unlock — copy & terminology

Research-grounded answer to two questions: is the lock/unlock/re-lock metaphor right, and what would a 5-star version say?

The one-line verdict. The research says your instinct is half right. "Locked / Unlocked" as a state is correct — keep it (it's the literal enterprise standard). What's wrong is using the lock as a verb ("Re-lock" to finish) and the word "staged" — both describe the plumbing, not what the user is trying to do. A 5-star version keeps the Locked/Unlocked state and renames the actions to the user's intent: Submit for approval, not Re-lock.

What the research says

1 · "Locked" for an approved record is the enterprise standard — not jargon keep

Salesforce — the reference point for B2B approval UX — locks a record when it's submitted for approval and after final approval, and calls the feature exactly that: "record locking." So a finance user who has seen any approval tool already reads "Locked" as "this is the official, protected record." Your Locked / Unlocked state vocabulary is right and worth keeping.

Salesforce Help — Record Locking in Flow Approval Processes, Specify Who Can Edit Locked Records

2 · But the lock is a consequence of submitting — never the action itself fix

In Salesforce the user's action is "Submit for Approval"; the record becomes locked automatically. Nobody clicks "re-lock." Your flow inverts this — the button to finish editing is Re-lock, which names the side-effect (the record locks) instead of the goal (the changes go for approval). NN/g's rule for command naming: a button should state the outcome the user wants, in their words — "Submit" alone is too vague; "Submit your order" is right. By that test Re-lock fails and Submit for approval wins. (Your confirm dialog already says "Submit for approval" — the trigger just doesn't match it.)

3 · The finance world already has a word for this — "pending approval" (maker-checker) adopt

This whole flow is the maker-checker / four-eyes pattern: one person proposes a change, another approves, and it doesn't take effect until then. Across finance tools the universal status label is "Pending Approval", and the change itself is a "proposed change." That's the vocabulary to lean on — it's what your buyer's accounting tools already use.

Maker-checker / 4-eyes — Aspire, Wallex, Volopay (status: "pending approval"; "the operation doesn't execute when the maker submits it; it sits pending until the checker approves")

4 · "Staged" is borrowed developer jargon — cut it cut

"Staged" comes from Git's staging area. It's invisible to engineers and opaque to everyone else. NN/g is explicit: avoid technical jargon and internal terminology; use plain language — it "creates barriers that slow users." The nearest plain-English idea your user already knows is Google Docs "Suggesting" mode: you propose changes, someone accepts or rejects them. So: staged → "proposed changes" / "changes that need approval."

NN/g — The 3 C's of Microcopy; Google Docs — Suggest edits (propose changes; owner accepts/rejects)

5 · In lease-land, an approved-then-changed term is a "correction / amendment" with an audit trail — the approval framing is domain-correct

Lease administration treats any change to an executed lease as a correction or amendment that must be approved, versioned, and audit-logged. So routing edits through approval isn't an odd software constraint to apologize for — it's exactly what a finance team expects. The copy can say so plainly and even sound more trustworthy for it.

Recommendation

  • Keep Locked / Unlocked as the record state — it's the enterprise standard and signals "official, protected record."
  • Stop using the lock as a verb to finish. The primary action is "Submit for approval" (it already is in your confirm dialog — just rename the trigger to match). The re-lock becomes a one-line explained consequence ("the lease stays locked while it's reviewed"). For the no-changes case, "Lock lease."
  • Delete "staged" everywhere → "proposed changes" / "changes need approval" / "pending approval" — the maker-checker words your buyer already knows.
  • Lead with intent, explain the rule once. "Unlock to edit" to open; a single calm sentence that changes need approval; never make the user decode the mechanism.
