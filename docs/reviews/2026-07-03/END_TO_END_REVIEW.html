End-to-End Review & Implementation Plan

Every page, edge function, migration, and flow in the repo was read and verified against the code — no assumptions. This document is the findings, the architecture verdict, and the plan to get LeaseIO to a product a warehouse manager, a finance lead, and a CFO can actually complete a lease through.

The verdict

Fix the repo. Do not start from scratch.

All 17 subsystem reviews reached the same verdict independently: fix in place. Not one recommended a rebuild — and after verifying their evidence, I agree.

Why this is a wiring problem, not a foundation problem

The hard parts of LeaseIO are done and done well: the policy resolver and approval-chain engine, the row-level-security fabric, the governance triggers that make locked leases immutable, the audit-trail convention, the Stripe webhook defenses, and the two-pass AI extraction pipeline. 1,357 of 1,357 tests pass. A rebuild would throw away the most expensive, most correct 70% of the product.

What is broken is the last mile: at least fifteen fully-built features are dead because one wire was never connected — a signature page no link points to, a nudge button behind a hardcoded false, notifications written without recipients so they never send, a negotiation panel unreachable because of one wrong early-return. And every backward arrow (send-backs, rejections) strands the lease permanently. The fix is weeks of disciplined wiring — not a rewrite.

2 · Every backward arrow is broken

You asked me to “handle the rejections or send backs.” Today, nobody can: a concept send-back supersedes every pending step and leaves zero pending rows with no resubmit mechanism (the dialog tells the approver “the submitter must resubmit” — no such flow exists). A signator send-back can never be recovered because re-advancing only re-stamps existing pending rows and none survive. rejected is a hard-terminal state though the architecture doc promises a revise loop. chain_violation’s only exit button fires a browser write the governance trigger always rejects. Every one of these strands the lease permanently.

3 · The product is silent

Approvals move, and nobody is told. The requestor is never notified of any outcome — not approval (“go get a quote”), not rejection, not send-back. The next sequential approver is never notified. The signature notification goes to a role nobody can hold. The unlock/change-set loop writes audit rows only. The one page that would show all of this (/app/notifications) is unreachable from navigation.

4 · Two of everything

Two approval systems (legacy threshold/roles + policy chains) with three rival config surfaces and undocumented precedence; two role systems (membership roles vs functional roles) colliding on one screen; five extraction pipelines (primary, retry, executed, amendment, audit-session) where retry diverges so badly it strands recovered leases outside the portfolio; two state vocabularies (legacy + chain) that the UI keeps confusing — the root cause of the negotiation-panel blocker is precisely an equivalence mapping between the two.

5 · Money and data-safety bugs

A short list of genuinely severe, verified issues that outrank everything else: account deletion destroys other tenants’ lease records (deletes by uploader ID across workspaces) and cancels zero Stripe subscriptions (deleted customers billed forever); plan switching double-bills (old subscription never cancelled); Starter is free forever on its default path (no checkout, no trial clock, no enforcement — the landing page’s “7-day free trial” is not implemented); unlimited free workspaces via a wide-open INSERT policy (each resets the AI quota); report PDF uploads blocked for everyone by a mis-bound storage RLS policy (filed as #18 six weeks ago, fix written, never applied).

6 · Code-complete but never deployed

A two-week-old queue of finished, reviewed work is inert because operator steps never ran: four edge-function redeploys (including the resolver — meaning Path-1 submission may be broken in production right now), four cron secrets, at least five never-scheduled crons (counter-signature reminders, delegate timers, stuck-chain detection, retention purge, notification e-mail dispatch), and the Stripe STOPs (live webhook, annual prices, Vault product, pack prices). Full checklist in Operator backlog.

Path 1 · Proposal

The pushback: three gates, not six touchpoints

You said it yourself: “this will lose interest fast if it is overcomplicated.” Your described cycle interrupts the manager four separate times (request, quote, contract, pre-CFO) plus the CFO plus finance. I recommend collapsing it to three named gates that preserve every control point you asked for.

GateQuestion it answersWho actsHow it maps to your steps
Gate 1 · Request“Should we shop for this at all?”ManagerYour steps 1–3. Already built; needs the “approved — go get quotes” notification and guidance.
Gate 2 · Quote“Can we commit at this price?”ManagerYour step 4. The one genuinely new gate: a quote document type + a lightweight approval checkpoint. Toggleable per approval template — small equipment leases can skip it.
Gate 3 · Signature“Do we bind the company?”Manager confirms → CFO signs · Finance is notified & watchingYour steps 5–7 merged. The manager’s contract check becomes the first sequential step of the signature stage (the engine already supports multi-step stages), the CFO’s attestation is the second, and Finance gets simultaneous visibility as watchers — notified on entry, full read access, no approval burden.

Why merge the contract check into the signature gate? In your version, the manager approves the contract, and then the same document goes to the CFO. Two ceremonies, one artifact, days of latency between them — and the stuck-chain data in this codebase (7-day detection threshold) says every extra interruption is where deals stall. As sequential steps of one gate, the manager and CFO act on the same screen contexts back-to-back, each step notifies the next automatically, and the requestor’s tracker shows one “Signature” stage instead of two confusing ones.

Send-backs, rejections, and nudges — the full contract

  • Every gate offers three actions: Approve · Send back (requires a note; returns the request to the requestor in an editable state whose primary button simply reads Submit — the resolver’s existing forceConceptReactivation mode does the chain reset) · Decline (terminal, reason required, requestor notified; one click duplicates the data into a fresh request so nothing is retyped).
  • Every transition notifies someone by name. The full matrix is in § Notification matrix — no state change is silent, ever.
  • Nudges: the already-built manual nudge (24-hour cooldown, server-enforced) surfaces on the requestor’s tracker and each queue card; the already-typed automatic day-2/5/10 reminders get their cron wired; stuck chains (7 days) escalate to admins via the Exceptions dashboard, which finally gets a nav entry.

Path 2 · Direct add

Direct add: review first, then post — with a real permission

Today, clicking Add lease runs the AI and drops the lease straight into the portfolio at lifecycle executed — counted in totals before anyone has reviewed a single extracted field, with no approval concept anywhere and no permission model (process_lease/index.ts:2517). That contradicts your stated contract: “user adds lease, gets abstracted, reviews fields, and submits for approval — unless they have permission to post without approval.”

The review workbench itself is in good shape (per-field confidence, tab-by-tab attestation, PDF verify). What’s missing is the landing state and the posting gate:

  1. Land in review, not the portfolio. Extraction completes into a pending_review state — invisible to portfolio totals, visible in a “Needs review” queue. (This also revives the dead NeedsReviewBanner and fixes the false “document missing” flag every direct-add currently gets on the Dashboard.)
  2. “Post without approval” is a functional role, not a checkbox column. Add direct_poster to workspace_roles — the same table every other approval capability lives in. Owner, workspace admins, and financial approvers qualify implicitly; anyone else can be granted it on the Members screen.
  3. One server action, two outcomes. A single post_direct action (service-role, so the governance trigger stays intact) checks the permission:
    • Has it → pending_review → active in one step, model-locked, audit row marked posted_without_approval: true. The UI button reads Post to portfolio.
    • Doesn’t → resolves an approval chain that enters at the signature stage (the document is already signed — running it through concept/quote gates would be nonsense), and the button reads Submit for approval.

Approvals · Setup

Approval setup: templates first, engine untouched

You called the current setup “overly complicated.” Verified: reaching the editor takes 5 clicks through a pass-through tab; the editor exposes ~13 concepts (priority, fallback flags, parallel groups, separation-of-duties overrides…); and four of its defaults are traps that later explode in the requestor’s face, not the admin’s.

The traps, concretely (all confirmed in code)

① Create one narrow rule (say, Real Estate only) → every other request type in the workspace hard-fails at submission with a 409, because “Active” defaults on, “fallback” defaults off, and the legacy path switches off the moment one active policy exists. ② Every rule defaults to priority 100, ties aren’t detected, and tied matches reject the submission outright — Duplicate-then-activate makes this the default outcome. ③ The editor’s flagship “AND at the same time” control writes data the engine runs sequentially (it keys parallelism on step_order, which the editor increments). ④ The “Try it on a sample request” dialog uses different logic than the live resolver — it says “matched” for exactly the configurations that will fail real submissions.

The engine underneath (policy matching → chain snapshot → frontier advancement) is sound and well-tested. So the redesign is a presentation layer:

  • Seed every new workspace with a working default policy — “Manager approves, owner signs,” fallback on — so a fresh workspace routes sanely from request #1 and the legacy path becomes unreachable for new workspaces.
  • Simple mode: three templates + two questions. Covers what an SMB finance team actually needs; writes ordinary approval_policies rows under the hood. Advanced mode remains the existing editor for the 5% who need per-department chains.
  • Guardrails at save time, not submission time: atomic policy+steps save (today a half-failed save strands an active zero-step rule that 409s everything it matches); tie detection; “no fallback exists” warning; “this role has no holders” warning; and a test dialog that calls the real resolver.
  • Fix the parallel encoding (same step_order, new parallel_group) with a one-time data repair for existing policies.
  • One configuration surface. The legacy “Approval Chain” card and the orphaned “Review Thresholds” setting fold into Approval Rules with a migration note — today three rival surfaces coexist and nothing tells the admin which one is live.

What a fresh workspace contains: column defaults and one owner-membership row. That’s all. No approval policy (so requests silently auto-approve until someone finds the roles screen), no functional roles, no departments, no alert rules, timezone hardcoded to America/New_York even though signup collects a timezone. That fix is Phase 0, not Phase 6.

Definition of done — how every phase exits

No phase is “done” on green tests alone. Each one exits through, in order: ① a rendered walkthrough of the affected user journeys — the app run against staging and clicked through as the real personas (requestor, approver, CFO, firm admin), with screenshots attached to the phase summary; ② the polish and layout review lanes on every touched surface, covering copy (labels like “Save Draft”, not “Save & finish later”), empty states, error states, and i18n coverage — not just the diff, the whole screen; ③ the standing reviewer sweep (code auditor, security scanner, integrity reviewer, test author) with Critical/High findings surfaced before any fix; and ④ docs reconciled in the same commit.

Two known blind spots from this review are folded into gate ①: workspace-switching state isolation (React Query cache bleed between workspaces) is exercised in every multi-workspace walkthrough, and the firm journey (create firm → invite member → open child workspace → switch back) is the mandatory exit test for the firm items in Phase 6.

Your call

Six decisions — all resolved 2026-07-04

All six were decided per the recommendation. Decision 2 was refined by the owner beyond the recommendation; its resolution below is the binding version, and it exposed a genuine contradiction between the two source-of-truth documents.

1 · Starter monetization — enforce it? Resolved — recommended

Today the default signup path never touches Stripe: no card, no trial clock, nothing server-side ever blocks a never-paying workspace, and the landing page’s “7-day free trial” is not implemented anywhere.

Recommend: checkout with 7-day trial at onboarding (card up front), grace-lock after — matches the landing copy you already published.

2 · The ASC 842 report vs. Hard Rule #1 Resolved — refined by owner

Root cause surfaced during resolution: the two source-of-truth documents contradict each other. CLAUDE.md Hard Rule #1 says “never add ASC 842 compliance features”; PRODUCT_STRATEGY.md (line 13) says LeaseIO “produces verified ASC 842 reports.” Phase 8 built to the strategy doc — which is how the calculation engine got shipped against the hard rule.

Owner’s ruling (2026-07-04): LeaseIO is the repository for ASC 842 information, never the calculator. Users enter their own ASC figures per lease — classification, discount rate, and the ROU-asset/liability numbers produced by their accounting tool — and LeaseIO stores them with attribution and reports them verbatim alongside the lease record, documents, and audit trail. Every LeaseIO-computed accounting output (PV of liability, straight-line expense, the five classification tests) comes out of the user-facing product. The section also gets a ground-up UX redesign — the owner’s verdict on the current one: not clean, simple, or easy to navigate. Both source documents are amended to this ruling in the same change, per the schema-and-docs discipline.

Scope: Phase 6 → “ASC data entry + reporting redesign” (promoted from a polish bullet to a named work item).

3 · Retire the legacy approval path for new workspaces? Resolved — recommended

Once every new workspace is seeded with a default policy, the legacy threshold/roles path becomes unreachable for them, and the three-way config confusion collapses. Existing legacy-routed leases finish on the old path; no data migration.

Recommend: yes — seed + retire for new, grandfather in-flight.

4 · Quote gate default — on or off? Resolved — recommended

Gate 2 is the piece of your ideal flow that adds a genuinely new interruption. As a template toggle it can default on (your described flow) or off (lighter default, teams opt in).

Recommend: on in the “Manager approves, CFO signs” template, off in “Manager only.”

5 · Departments: free text or picklist? Resolved — recommended

Approval rules can match on department, but the request form collects free text — “Ops” ≠ “Operations”, so department-scoped rules silently misroute. The workspace already has a department_options config column that the form ignores.

Recommend: picklist from workspace settings, seeded at creation.

6 · Rejection semantics — terminal or revisable? Resolved — recommended

The architecture doc promises a revise loop after rejection; the code makes rejection hard-terminal. Two coherent options: keep rejection terminal (with reason + one-click “duplicate into a new request”), or add a revisable rejected state.

Recommend: keep terminal + duplicate — simpler mental model, preserves the audit trail, and “Send back” already covers the fix-and-return case.
